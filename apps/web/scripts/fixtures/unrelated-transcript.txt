CONTROL FIXTURE — not a real client session.

This transcript exists to falsify the extraction check. It is deliberately the
HARD case rather than the easy one: it is business talk, in the same register as
a real coaching call, between two people who never agree to do anything. A
control made of small talk would prove very little, because any extractor can
tell a cricket score from a commitment. The question this fixture asks is
narrower and much more useful: can the extractor tell "describing the work" from
"committing to an action"?

If any commitment comes back from this file, the extractor is pattern-matching
on business vocabulary rather than reading for agreed actions.

---

SPEAKER A: So how long have you been in the game now?

SPEAKER B: Coming up on eleven years. Started out doing carpet cleaning out of
the back of a station wagon, and it just sort of grew from there. These days
it's mostly commercial. Offices, a couple of shopping centres, some strata work
here and there.

SPEAKER A: And the team?

SPEAKER B: Six full-time, plus casuals when it gets busy. Which is most of
summer, honestly. Storm season is our whole year, really. You do sixty per cent
of your turnover in about four months and then you sit around wondering where
everyone went.

SPEAKER A: That's the trap with this industry, isn't it. Everyone's flat out or
everyone's starving.

SPEAKER B: Exactly right. And the insurance work makes it worse, because you're
waiting on assessors, waiting on approvals, and meanwhile your blokes are on the
clock. I've had jobs sit for three weeks waiting for someone to tick a box.

SPEAKER A: How do you handle the quoting side?

SPEAKER B: Badly, if I'm honest. It's all in my head. I've been doing it long
enough that I can walk into a job and know roughly what it's worth, but that
doesn't help anyone else. If I'm away, the quotes just don't go out.

SPEAKER A: A lot of operators are in that boat. The knowledge is real, it's just
not written down anywhere.

SPEAKER B: That's it. My old man was the same. He ran a plastering business for
thirty years and when he retired the whole thing just evaporated, because none
of it existed outside his head. There was nothing to sell.

SPEAKER A: Did that change how you think about your own setup?

SPEAKER B: I think about it. Whether I do anything about it is another question.
You get busy. The urgent stuff wins every time.

SPEAKER A: It usually does.

SPEAKER B: The other thing is equipment. We've got about eighty grand tied up in
dehus and air movers, and half of it sits in the shed nine months of the year.
I've thought about hiring it out to the smaller operators, but then you're in the
hire business, and I don't want to be in the hire business.

SPEAKER A: Fair enough.

SPEAKER B: Anyway. That's the state of things. Some of it works, some of it
doesn't, and most days I'm too flat out to tell which is which.

SPEAKER A: That sounds about right for this trade.

SPEAKER B: It is what it is. Good to talk it through, anyway.

SPEAKER A: Any time.
